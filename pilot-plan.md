# Pilot Plan

## Executive Summary

The first Quvvatla pilot is planned for Tashkent, Uzbekistan.

The pilot is designed to validate the complete operating model of a mobile EV charging service in real urban conditions.

Quvvatla is built around one central principle:

**EV charging should adapt to people's lives — not the other way around.**

The pilot will therefore test not only whether mobile EV charging is technically feasible, but whether there is real customer demand, whether the service can operate reliably in Tashkent traffic conditions, whether the scheduling and digital processes work correctly, whether the mobile unit has sufficient operating capacity, and whether the commercial model can become economically sustainable.

The pilot will also test whether customers value the convenience, flexibility, and time-saving benefits of the service enough to use it repeatedly.

## 1. Pilot Objectives

The pilot is intended to answer the following core questions:

* Is there real demand for mobile EV charging?
* Which customer segments demonstrate the strongest demand?
* In which locations, days, and time periods is demand highest?
* How many charging requests can one mobile unit realistically serve?
* Can the service maintain predictable response times under real traffic conditions?
* Can the scheduling and service management system handle multiple orders effectively?
* Does the mobile unit have sufficient operating capacity for the planned number of charging sessions and travel between customers?
* How much time is required to recharge the mobile unit itself?
* Which tariff and time-slot options are most attractive to customers?
* Do actual service costs support the planned pricing model?
* Can the operating team deliver the service consistently and efficiently?
* Do customers perceive sufficient convenience and time-saving value to use the service again?
* Is the model suitable for further development and scale?

## 2. Demand Validation

The pilot will measure real customer behavior rather than relying only on stated interest.

Key indicators may include:

* number of charging requests;
* number of completed orders;
* request-to-order conversion;
* cancelled orders;
* reasons for cancellation;
* repeat usage;
* demand by customer segment;
* demand by geographic area;
* demand by day of the week;
* demand by time of day;
* demand by tariff;
* demand by selected charging time;
* advance bookings versus immediate requests;
* requests that cannot be accepted because of service capacity.

The objective is to understand where, when, and for whom mobile EV charging creates the strongest demand.

## 3. Customer Time and Convenience

Customer experience will be evaluated as one part of the pilot, but not as the only measure of success.

Quvvatla's key customer proposition is to reduce the amount of time and effort EV owners spend managing the charging process.

The pilot may assess:

* whether customers believe the service reduced the time they normally spend on EV charging;
* whether customers avoided travelling to a fixed charging location;
* whether customers avoided waiting for an available charging slot;
* whether charging was easier to integrate into their normal daily routine;
* whether the customer would use the service again;
* which situations create the greatest value for the customer;
* whether the customer considers the selected tariff acceptable for the convenience provided.

Actual repeat usage and purchasing behavior will be considered more important than positive feedback alone.

## 4. Order Processing Validation

The pilot will measure how efficiently customer requests move through the service process.

This may include:

* time from request submission to system registration;
* time required to confirm an order;
* time between confirmation and dispatch;
* time required to assign the order;
* manual intervention required per order;
* errors in order processing;
* changes or cancellations after confirmation;
* processing time during normal operating periods;
* processing time during peak demand periods.

The purpose is to identify bottlenecks and determine how much operational effort is required to manage each order.

## 5. Logistics and Travel Time Validation

Tashkent traffic conditions are an important part of the operating model.

The pilot will measure:

* distance between the mobile unit and the customer;
* planned travel time;
* actual travel time;
* difference between estimated and actual arrival time;
* travel time during normal traffic conditions;
* travel time during morning peak hours;
* travel time during evening peak hours;
* impact of road closures;
* impact of road construction;
* impact of temporary traffic restrictions;
* impact of accidents and unexpected congestion;
* number of orders delayed because of traffic conditions;
* number of orders rescheduled because of traffic conditions;
* number of orders cancelled because of service delays.

## 6. ETA Accuracy and Schedule Recovery

The pilot will specifically test how accurately the service can predict arrival times.

Key indicators may include:

* predicted ETA versus actual arrival time;
* average ETA deviation;
* ETA accuracy during normal traffic;
* ETA accuracy during peak traffic;
* ETA accuracy during road disruptions.

The pilot will also test the ability of the operational or digital system to recover after delays.

For example:

* if one order is delayed, can the following orders be rescheduled correctly?
* how much does one delayed order affect the remaining daily schedule?
* can customers be informed about schedule changes in time?
* how quickly can the service recover from an unexpected disruption?

This will help determine whether Quvvatla can offer customers a predictable service under real city conditions.

## 7. Mobile Unit Operating Capacity

The pilot will measure the real operating capacity of the mobile charging unit.

This may include:

* available operating capacity at the beginning of the shift;
* energy delivered per charging session;
* remaining capacity after each charging session;
* travel distance between orders;
* energy or operating resources required for travel;
* number of charging sessions completed before the mobile unit requires recharging;
* total number of orders completed per shift;
* operating capacity under different customer and geographic scenarios;
* impact of long travel distances on service capacity;
* impact of customer charging requirements on the number of orders that can be completed.

The objective is to determine the realistic daily service capacity of one mobile unit.

## 8. Mobile Unit Recharging Validation

The pilot will also measure the time and operational impact of recharging the mobile unit itself.

Key areas may include:

* actual time required for recharging;
* partial versus full recharge time;
* frequency of recharging;
* optimal time of day for recharging;
* possibility of recharging between customer orders;
* downtime caused by recharging;
* impact of recharging on the number of daily customer orders;
* difference between planned and actual recharging requirements.

Different operating scenarios may be compared, for example:

* overnight recharging;
* partial daytime recharging;
* recharge between demand peaks.

The objective is to identify a practical operating cycle that maximizes service availability.

## 9. Scheduling and Digital System Validation

If digital tools are included in the MVP, the pilot will test whether they support real service operations correctly.

Potential validation areas include:

* customer request submission;
* location capture;
* order confirmation;
* service scheduling;
* allocation of orders;
* route planning;
* ETA calculation;
* order status updates;
* charging session records;
* customer notifications;
* price calculation;
* tariff selection;
* time-slot selection;
* handling of rescheduling and cancellations;
* operational monitoring;
* data collection.

The pilot should identify where the digital process works correctly and where manual intervention, redesign, or further development is required.

## 10. Time-Slot Demand Validation

The pilot will test how customers choose charging times.

Data may include:

* preferred charging times;
* most requested time slots;
* least requested time slots;
* morning demand;
* daytime demand;
* evening demand;
* weekday versus weekend demand;
* advance-booking patterns;
* urgent versus scheduled charging demand.

This information will help identify peak periods and understand how many mobile units may eventually be required at different times of day.

## 11. Tariff Validation

The pilot may test different tariff or service options without publicly disclosing the detailed commercial model.

The objective is to understand:

* which tariff options customers choose;
* whether customers prefer scheduled or priority service;
* whether customers are willing to select a less convenient time in exchange for a different price;
* whether customers value faster or priority service;
* which tariffs generate the strongest demand;
* how tariff selection changes by customer segment;
* how tariff selection changes by location and time of day.

The pilot will also compare customer demand with the real cost of delivering each type of service.

## 12. Demand Heatmap

Pilot data may be used to develop an operational demand map combining:

* geographic location;
* time of day;
* day of week;
* customer segment;
* selected tariff;
* selected charging time;
* urgency;
* service duration.

This will help identify geographic and time-based demand clusters and support future fleet planning.

## 13. Technical Performance Validation

The pilot will monitor the reliability of charging operations.

Potential indicators may include:

* successful charging sessions;
* failed charging sessions;
* charging duration;
* service interruptions;
* technical errors;
* compatibility issues;
* operational downtime;
* actual performance compared with expected performance;
* frequency and type of technical incidents.

The objective is to determine whether the technical solution can operate consistently enough for commercial service.

## 14. Unit Economics and Cost Validation

A critical purpose of the pilot is to compare planned economics with real operating costs.

The pilot should measure, internally:

* cost of energy;
* travel-related costs;
* staff costs;
* operating costs;
* recharging costs;
* maintenance-related costs;
* cost per completed order;
* cost per charging session;
* average revenue per order;
* revenue by tariff;
* contribution margin by order;
* impact of travel distance on cost;
* impact of traffic delays on cost;
* impact of mobile-unit downtime on cost;
* impact of low utilization on economics;
* minimum service utilization required for the model to become economically viable.

The pilot will test whether customer pricing can realistically cover the cost of service delivery and support a sustainable commercial model.

Detailed cost values and commercial calculations will remain confidential and will not be published in this repository.

## 15. Relationship Between Geography, Time, Tariff and Economics

The pilot should evaluate the relationship between key operating variables.

For example:

**Customer location → travel time → service duration → number of possible daily orders → cost per order → required tariff → customer demand at that tariff.**

Another example:

**Selected time slot → traffic conditions → ETA accuracy → operational cost → service capacity → customer satisfaction.**

This analysis will help identify which types of orders are operationally and commercially attractive.

## 16. Team and Operational Workflow Validation

The pilot will also test how effectively the Quvvatla team operates as one system.

Areas to evaluate may include:

* order intake;
* order confirmation;
* scheduling;
* dispatch;
* driver or operator coordination;
* charging operation;
* customer communication;
* technical support;
* payment or order closure;
* incident handling.

The pilot should identify:

* delays between operational steps;
* unclear responsibilities;
* duplicated work;
* communication failures;
* excessive manual intervention;
* staffing requirements;
* process bottlenecks;
* training needs.

The objective is to determine whether the operational workflow is reliable, efficient, and scalable.

## 17. Service Capacity and Utilization

The pilot will measure how much of the mobile unit's available operating time is productively used.

Potential indicators include:

* orders per shift;
* charging sessions per day;
* utilization rate;
* travel time as a percentage of the shift;
* charging time as a percentage of the shift;
* waiting or idle time;
* recharging downtime;
* cancelled capacity;
* unused capacity during low-demand periods.

This will help determine the practical productivity of one mobile unit.

## 18. Pilot Success Criteria

The pilot should produce enough evidence to make a structured decision after completion.

The final evaluation may use three possible outcomes:

### GO

Proceed toward broader commercial deployment if the pilot demonstrates:

* meaningful customer demand;
* acceptable repeat usage;
* reliable operations;
* manageable logistics;
* sufficient mobile-unit capacity;
* workable scheduling;
* acceptable service reliability;
* evidence of positive unit economics or a realistic path toward them;
* clear customer value.

### ADJUST

Continue development but modify the model if demand exists while significant operational, technical, pricing, scheduling, or economic problems remain.

### NO-GO / REASSESS

Reconsider the current model if the pilot shows:

* insufficient demand;
* consistently poor service economics;
* unacceptable travel or response times;
* insufficient mobile-unit capacity;
* major technical limitations;
* insufficient customer willingness to pay;
* operational complexity that prevents sustainable scaling.

Exact commercial thresholds will remain confidential.

## 19. Expected Pilot Outcome

The pilot is intended to provide evidence for decisions regarding:

* real market demand;
* priority customer segments;
* service areas;
* operating hours;
* tariff structure;
* time-slot strategy;
* fleet requirements;
* mobile-unit capacity;
* recharge strategy;
* digital system requirements;
* logistics and dispatch processes;
* staffing and team structure;
* customer experience;
* unit economics;
* commercial launch readiness;
* future scale.

## Confidentiality

This public pilot plan intentionally does not disclose:

* detailed technical architecture;
* energy system specifications;
* equipment configuration;
* supplier and technology partner information;
* detailed pricing;
* commercial terms;
* actual unit economics;
* proprietary scheduling logic;
* dispatch algorithms;
* detailed operating procedures;
* implementation logic.

These elements remain confidential and will be developed, tested, and documented separately during the MVP and pilot stages.
